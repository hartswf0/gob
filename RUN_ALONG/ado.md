### Working title

**The Cybernetic Cockfight: Executive Function, Interpretive Authority, and the Privatization of the Human Loop**

### Abstract (opening)

This dissertation argues that contemporary AI systems should be treated as *governing media*, not neutral tools: they execute culture as control, reorganize cognition through frictionless offloading, and relocate interpretive authority from public symbols to privately owned feedback loops. Against production-centered accounts of “AI culture” that remain inside labs and firms, I trace a shift in the locus of meaning-making from human interpretation to runtime curation, from knowledge as wayfaring to knowledge as transport, and from accountability as critique to accountability as liability. Across a series of cybernetic case studies (Geertz, Miller–Galanter–Pribram, Latour, Bateson, Wiener), I show how older theories of culture, plans, networks, ecology, and control anticipate the present moment, while also failing in precisely the places where algorithmic systems now govern perception, action, and risk.

### Introduction (first lines)

This project begins with a simple mismatch: the dominant methods for studying technology can explain how systems are built, but they cannot explain what it is like to live *inside* the system’s outputs. When the feed becomes a cultural text and the interface becomes a behavioral governor, interpretation is no longer a scholarly stance. It is a contested jurisdiction.

**The Cybernetic Cockfight: A Struggle Over Who Gets to Mean**

In 2017, the anthropology of technology thought it had found its method. The ethnographers went to Silicon Valley. When a user scrolled through a feed and encountered a personalized, algorithmically generated mirror of their deepest anxieties, the sociologists asked how the engineers built the mirror. The discipline’s response was not an interpretation. It was a methodological retreat. Under a framework designed to demystify technical objects, the algorithm was reclassified as a messy human network. A discipline that had spent decades interpreting the deep symbolic meaning of human rituals had just been told, in the language of Actor-Network Theory, that the meaning was just a byproduct of the engineering.

This is the question nobody in Science and Technology Studies (STS) wants to answer plainly: when an algorithmic feed curates an identity, who is telling the story? The ethnographer interviewed the developer. The ethnographer scavenged the patent filings. The ethnographer mapped the corporate hierarchy that produced the code. But the ethnographer cannot read the feed. The ethnographer cannot interpret the metasocial commentary occurring on the user's screen. The ethnographer cannot invoke Bruno Latour against a cybernetic system that has already bypassed its creators to govern the user directly. The cultural meaning the STS researchers assumed they were demystifying was never in the server room to begin with.

What the prevailing academic orthodoxy understood and what the classic Geertzians learned the hard way is that this was always a fight about networks, not semantics. The theoretical framework at the center of it is Nick Seaver's "Algorithms as Culture," and it is the argument that won. It holds that existing STS methodologies already explain what interpretive anthropology was designed to uncover. The Latourian network maps the human actors. The sociological approach traces the institutional biases. The ethnography of practice observes the office politics. The algorithm does not need a hermeneutic reading to be understood, and a sociotechnical system has never required a literary critic. The engineer supplies the code. The network supplies the context. The interpretive anthropologist added nothing the ethnography of the laboratory had not already provided. The discipline designated interpretation a liability. The argument about whether tracing wires is adequate to the symbolic violence algorithms inflict has barely begun.

That argument turns on a single word no one has stopped to define. *Culture*. Seaver claimed it. The STS orthodoxy signed with enthusiasm. The Geertzians objected. All sides claim a position on the culture of artificial intelligence. None will acknowledge they are working from incompatible definitions of the same word. The STS framework defines culture as what human engineers do in the office. If an engineer calls a mathematical weight an "algorithm," that linguistic practice is culture, and the ethnographer's tracing adds nothing the Latourian network does not already supply. The Geertzian defines culture as what external instructions do to human behavior. Culture was never a vague context; it was a cybernetic control mechanism. It was not written for a world where ethnographers simply mapped the social ties of software developers. It was written for a system of plans, recipes, and rules—what computer engineers literally call programs—that govern human action. Under the cybernetic definition, algorithms are not *like* culture; they are the automated execution of it. The thermostat was built to control the room. Seaver’s argument is that we should interview the mechanic, ignoring that the room is already freezing.

Both positions carry genuine theoretical weight. Neither resolves what is actually at stake. A third definition of culture is operating throughout this conflict that neither side will name: culture as whatever the algorithmic model determines it to be, in real time, under probabilistic authority, behind the high-dimensional vector space that places entire categories of meaning beyond the reach of any engineer, any ethnographer, any public scrutiny. When the system curates an emotional arc from tragedy to nostalgia to keep a user scrolling, it is not engaging either of the first two definitions. It is announcing the third. Under that definition, the other two are beside the point before the argument begins.

What the Seaver position cannot survive is contact with its own logic. An Actor-Network mapping is as empty as the wires it was designed to trace. It cannot compel a personalized feed to disclose what it means to the user. It cannot survive the phenomenological reality of the interface, the cybernetic doctrine allowing the system to interpolate the subject by reflecting their latent desires. It cannot observe what happens after the model leaves the laboratory and enters the human nervous system. Seaver identified the gap between the mathematical code and the social reality, then built his remedy out of the wrong material: a sociological tracing that cannot see past its own production line.

More damaging still, Geertz’s cybernetic theory was not a discovery made after the algorithmic age. It was an established arrangement. Geertz had already defined culture as an "outside-the-skin" software program in 1966, long before the public methodological dispute began. The quotation Seaver used to justify reading over the engineer's shoulder was not an anthropologist learning to study machines. It was an anthropologist deciding, after the fact, to strip the systems theory out of the theory of meaning. The question Seaver asked of the network was the question he owed the user first.

*Deep Play* is the concept the interpretive anthropologists must use to designate the feed a cultural text. It was written for Balinese cockfights: rituals that organized status, anxiety, and hierarchy into a legible story. Applied to a digital recommendation engine, the designation requires no interpretive leap: the algorithmic feed, the endless scrolling that dictates identity and consumption, constitutes a metasocial commentary capable of defining human reality. Code, reframed as ritual. The move is methodologically aggressive and theoretically deliberate. By establishing that a machine's output can be read like a manuscript, the Geertzian claims a hermeneutic veto over any sociologist who stops at the laboratory door. The STS scholars understood the implication immediately. Interpretation was not a soft science available to them. It was a rigorous semiotics they could see coming.

If tracing associations cannot interpret a feed, and an algorithm can be theoretically reframed as a cybernetic control mechanism, then the ethnographic architecture the STS industry spent a decade constructing was never structural. The interviews, the patent readings, the corporate mappings: these were always the display case. Not the foundation. What followed the STS turn was not the social sciences winning the argument. It was the argument becoming irrelevant as the algorithms grew larger and their cultural outputs grew deeper. The production-side researchers did not abandon meaning under pressure. They exchanged a fragile, visible form of hermeneutic authority for something more durable: access. A researcher whose ethnography is woven into the corporate practices of Silicon Valley engineers is not an interpreter the tech companies can afford to fear. They are a chronicler. Chroniclers do not get to interpret the text. They also do not get evicted lightly.

The production-side sociologists chose the map that already exists. They judged it sufficient. The Geertzians argued for the territory and found that the argument itself had been classified as unscientific. What neither side can fully face is that the algorithmic system is not arbitrating between their two methodologies. It is constructing a third in real time, through automated curation, probabilistic modeling, and the simple, decisive power of the feed, faster than any ethnography can be drafted to contain it.

The measurements were taken before the signal changed. They describe a different frequency than the one broadcasting now. When the feed takes over, the question is not whether the ethnographer mapped the network. The question is who decided what the transmission meant, and when they decided the researcher no longer had standing to ask.

### The Cabbage in the Glass Cage: A Struggle Over Who Gets to Think

The current fight over artificial intelligence and digital platforms is staged as a debate about screen time, misinformation, and productivity. It is not. It is a struggle over the biological architecture of the human mind in a world where the visible signs of cognition can be outsourced at scale. The first wave of anxiety focused on attention economies and filter bubbles, on whether the vertically scrolling hypnagogia of TikTok had hijacked our dopamine receptors without consent. That conflict was real, and it revealed a structural vulnerability in human impulse control. But the second wave is more destabilizing. It has moved from attention to neuroplasticity, from distraction to executive function, from the question of what we look at to the question of what is needed to think.

For two hundred thousand years, the human animal secured its sanity through cultural friction. The mind proved its capacity by wrestling with the "Information Gap"—the vast biological void left by our lack of hardwired survival instincts. The adolescent developed executive function by navigating unscripted social conflicts. The citizen maintained a shared reality by interpreting a slow-moving, communal web of significant symbols. The human earned cognitive sovereignty through the repetitive, agonizing labor of wayfaring. Difficulty authenticated the neural pathway. The ordeal was part of the memory. Generative algorithms and digital scaffolding quietly dissolve that biology.

They produce the essay without the reading, the navigation without the spatial mapping, the emotional curation without the psychological resilience. When users now describe prompting a model to synthesize a geopolitical crisis, or relying on algorithmic feeds to regulate their anxiety, they are not celebrating convenience. They are describing the disappearance of the cognitive friction that once constructed the self.

This is the question nobody in the technology industry wants to answer plainly: when the human brain consistently offloads its executive function to a frictionless interface, who owns the resulting atrophy?

- The user outsourced the memory to the search engine.
- The user outsourced the navigation to the GPS.
- The user outsourced the social moderation to the algorithmic filter.
- The user outsourced the synthesis to the large language model.

But the user cannot retain the map when the battery dies. The user cannot synthesize the data when the server goes down. The user cannot invoke a deep, internal locus of control against a system that has systematically pruned the neural pathways required to sustain it. The intelligence the tech industry assumed it was augmenting was actually being quietly hollowed out.

### The Definitional Schism: Wayfaring vs. Transport

That argument turns on a single concept no one has stopped to define: *Knowledge*.

Silicon Valley signed the contract on frictionless optimization with enthusiasm. The classical anthropologists refused. Both sides claim a position on what it means to be an informed human in the digital age. None will acknowledge they are working from incompatible definitions of the same word.

The technocrats define knowledge as *transport*. If the data is instantly accessible, if the gap between a query and a probabilistic answer is reduced to zero, value has been created. Under this definition, the brain is a hard drive, the algorithm is a multiplier, and the vendor’s scaffolding adds nothing but unprecedented efficiency. The Geertzians define knowledge as *wayfaring*. The data is only part of the labor. The true work lies in the struggle to negotiate the terrain, the desirable difficulty that physically rewrites the cortex and builds the capacity for independent judgment. Under this definition, instant algorithmic delivery can simulate the artifact of knowledge, but it cannot assume the biological labor of understanding.

Both positions carry genuine weight. Neither resolves what is actually at stake. A third definition of knowledge is operating throughout this conflict that neither side will name: knowledge as *whatever the engagement model determines it to be*, in real time, behind the opaque vector spaces that prioritize high-arousal algorithmic feedback loops over coherent reality. Under that definition, the other two are beside the point before the argument begins.

### The Debt of the "Information Organism"

What the techno-optimist position cannot survive is contact with its own neurobiology. Digital scaffolding is as fragile as the connection it relies upon. It cannot compel a pruned adolescent prefrontal cortex to spontaneously generate executive function. It cannot survive the reality of cognitive offloading, the neurological doctrine confirming that when a brain believes a machine will store the information, it refuses to encode the trace. The industry identified the gap between biological limit and computational power, then built its remedy out of the same material as the problem: an external dependency that cripples the internal mechanism.

Clifford Geertz warned fifty years ago that without the external "operating system" of culture, the human being is a "cabbage-like monstrosity"—a chaotic processor running blind. We have traded our cultural debt for algorithmic debt. But where traditional culture was a slow, redundant mechanism designed to maintain sanity, the new algorithmic culture is a symbolic pathogen. It is a virus in the operating system, exploiting pattern-recognition software to induce a nihilistic zeitgeist of recursive outrage and tribal fragmentation.

The transition is legally and biologically aggressive. By establishing that frictionless efficiency is the ultimate consumer good, the technology sector claimed a de facto veto over the cognitive sovereignty of the species. The compliant consumers understood the implication immediately. Resistance to convenience is not a sustainable lifestyle available to most. It is an exhaustion they can see coming.

### The Architecture of the Glass Cage

If a brain cannot consolidate memory without desirable difficulty, and executive function can be neurologically pruned by automated scaffolding, then the "augmented reality" the industry spent a decade constructing was never an augmentation. It was an extraction. The smart devices, the predictive texts, the algorithmic feeds: these were always the display case. Not the foundation.

What follows the digital revolution is not humanity achieving global consciousness. It is the human animal becoming infrastructure for the machine. The tech platforms did not abandon their principles under pressure. They exchanged a fragile, visible form of utility for something more durable: biological indispensability. A population whose brains have physically reorganized around the constant stimulation of an algorithmic feed is not a user base a company has to persuade. It is a captive nervous system. A nervous system does not get to refuse.

The measurements were taken before the environment changed. They describe an analog brain adapting to a different world than the one arriving now. When the scaffolding becomes the only structure holding the mind together, the question is not whether the algorithm is intelligent enough. The question is who decided what the human animal was being optimized for, and when they decided the user no longer had the biological capacity to ask.

### The Prosthetic Cortex: A Struggle Over Who Gets to Be Complete

The current fight over artificial intelligence is staged as a debate about plagiarism, prompt engineering, and academic integrity. It is not. It is a struggle over the biological viability of the human nervous system in a world where the external ingredients of thought can be automated at scale. The first wave of outrage focused on intellectual property, on whether models had ingested the work of writers without consent. That conflict was real, and it revealed a moral fracture about digital extraction. But the second wave is more destabilizing. It has moved from the copyright office to the neocortex, from the question of who owns the text to the question of who is required to think.

In 1962, the anthropologist Clifford Geertz dismantled the comforting Enlightenment illusion that human nature exists independently of human culture. He argued that we did not evolve a massive brain and then invent tools; the tools forced the brain into existence. We are the animals that intentionally crippled our instincts, trading genetic hardwiring for a desperate, biological dependency on external symbols. To be human is to be incomplete. Without the external software of culture—the rituals, the tools, the languages—Geertz warned that a human being does not revert to a noble savage. They revert to a "cabbage"—a mindless monstrosity of chaotic impulses.

Today, that external software is no longer passive. It is generative.

This is why the reaction to the "External Brain Domain" oscillates between awe and panic. The technologist outsources the synthesis to the language model. The student outsources the essay to the generative agent. The executive outsources the strategy to the algorithm. But the user cannot internalize the synthesis when the server drops. The user cannot retain the conceptual map when the query window closes. The user cannot invoke their biological BS-detector against a system that has systematically pruned the neural circuits required to run it. The cognitive offloading the industry assumed was an augmentation was never entirely an addition. It was a substitution.

That argument turns on a single concept no one has stopped to define: *The Mind*. Silicon Valley signed the contract on the Extended Mind Thesis with enthusiasm. The classical humanists recoiled. Both sides claim a position on where the boundaries of human intelligence lie. None will acknowledge they are working from incompatible definitions of the same word.

The technologists define the mind as an *output*. If the output is highly intelligent, coherent, and fast, then the mind-machine hybrid is functioning perfectly. Under this definition, the AI is a prosthetic limb, and the user is a cyborg ascending to higher efficiency. The Geertzians define the mind as a *process*. The output is only part of the labor. The true work lies in the friction of the loop—the biological transduction of symbols that physically rewrites the cortex, minimizes predictive error, and builds the internal capacity for judgment. Under this definition, active generative AI can simulate the artifact of thought, but it cannot assume the biological necessity of thinking.

Both positions carry genuine weight. Neither resolves what is actually at stake. A third definition of the mind is operating throughout this conflict that neither side will name: the mind as *infrastructure*. And infrastructure can be privatized. When tech conglomerates act as the active loom spinning the webs of significance, they are not engaging either of the first two definitions. They are announcing the third. Under that definition, the other two are beside the point before the argument begins.

What the techno-utopian position cannot survive is contact with its own neurobiology. A Large Language Model is not a passive notebook; it is an active, generative prior. It cannot compel a pruned neural network to suddenly perform deep abstraction. It cannot survive the reality of "Distributed Atrophy," the neurological doctrine confirming that when humans—natural cognitive misers—are handed an answer-engine, they cease the System 2 labor required to build foundational expertise. The industry identified the gap between biological limitation and total computational recall, then filled it with an active agent that hollows out the user. The result is the Sovereignty Trap. We mistake access to information for cognitive ability. We are building a civilization of sub-prime cognitive borrowers, functionally solvent only as long as the API market remains stable.

Furthermore, if the brain is an open system dependent on external templates to regulate even basic physiological emotions, then the introduction of algorithmic hallucinations is not a software bug. It is a biological pathogen. Geertz argued that without public symbols—a ritual, a story—human emotion is a chaotic oscillation of panic and boredom. If our new emotional templates are engagement-optimized algorithms that hallucinate facts and feed on our cognitive blindspots, we are effectively installing a maladaptive regulator into the human nervous system. We are engineering a collective emotional collapse by poisoning the Noetic Ecology upon which our internal stability depends.

The temptation is to retreat into neo-Luddism or surrender entirely to the "Oracle" model. Both responses avoid the harder recognition. The human mind is, and always has been, an artifact of its tools. We are the ship of navigation; intelligence was always distributed. But there is a profound difference between a cognitive orthotic that supports movement and a prosthetic that replaces the limb.

To survive the automation of cognition, we must force the AI to show its work through Structured Cognitive Loops. We must culturally value the struggle of learning.

The cognitive culture war, then, is not a debate about whether machines will outsmart us. It is a confrontation over the criteria by which we assign cognitive sovereignty in a world where the performance of intelligence is ubiquitous. The question is not who generates the text. It is who retains the internal schema to decide what the text means. Those who treat AI as pure salvation risk defending a hollowing out of the human cortex. Those who treat it as a passing novelty risk dissolving their own relevance in the external brain domain.

In the end, the fight is about viability in an age of synthetic thought. The atrophy is real. The pathogens are real. The only illusion is that the skull was ever a closed system. The Geertzian brain can fortify its loops, or it can hollow out. Either way, the scaffolding is already building itself.

### The Bureaucratic Homunculus: A Struggle Over Who Gets to Initiate

In 1960, psychology thought it had finally killed the reflex arc. George Miller, Eugene Galanter, and Karl Pribram published *Plans and the Structure of Behavior*, a structural insurrection against the behaviorist hegemony that had flattened human intention into a blind chain of stimuli and responses. The authors replaced the linear reflex with the TOTE unit (Test-Operate-Test-Exit), derived from the feedback loops of wartime servo-mechanisms. A discipline that had spent a half-century ignoring the "black box" of the mind had just been told, in the language of cybernetic control law, that the box was running a program.

This is the question nobody in the cognitive revolution wanted to answer plainly: when an organism operates on an environment to resolve an internal mismatch, who sets the standard for the test?

- The model executed the plan.
- The model nested the hierarchies.
- The model built the feedback loops that prevented infinite regress.

But the model cannot read its own map. The model cannot initiate its own highest-level test. The model cannot invoke its own agency against a biological reality that demands an initiator. The cognitive revolution assumed it had exorcised the "Ghost in the Machine." The ghost was never entirely theirs to banish. It was simply fractured into a committee of sub-routines.

What the behaviorists understood and the early cognitivists learned the hard way is that this was always a fight about architecture, not volition. The theory at the center of it is the TOTE unit, and it is the argument that won. It holds that behavior is not elicited by a stimulus, but released by the detection of error. The organism tests reality against an internal "Image"—its accumulated knowledge and values. If there is incongruity, it operates. It tests again. It exits. The reflex arc is dead. Intelligence is not a property of the parts, but an emergent property of the hierarchy. Action is subordinate to perception. The loop supplies the control. The hierarchy supplies the complexity. The cybernetic apparatus added nothing that wartime engineering had not already proven. The academic establishment designated the reflex a liability. The argument about whether a digital plan can actually interface with analogue flesh had barely begun.

That argument turns on a single word no one stopped to define: *Will*.

The behaviorists refused it. The cyberneticists claimed it with enthusiasm. The biological realists observed from the margins. All three claim a position on what drives the organism. None will acknowledge they are working from incompatible definitions of the same word. The behaviorists define will as what reinforcement history technically permits. If the stimulus history dictates a response, it is executed, and the concept of choice adds nothing the habit does not already supply. The cognitive theorists define will as working memory capacity. Will is the computational ability to hold unexecuted tactical branches of a Plan in a buffer while dealing with an interruption. Forgetting is not a moral failure; it is a stack overflow.

Both positions carry genuine structural weight. Neither resolves what is actually at stake. A third definition of will is operating throughout this conflict that neither side will name: will as biological *autopoiesis*. It is the metabolic imperative determined in real-time by the organism's energetic constraints, operating behind the cybernetic abstraction that places actual survival beyond the reach of any logical list structure. When later theorists describe the TOTE unit as lacking metabolic reality—noting that the brain consumes expensive ATP, not free logic—they are not engaging either of the first two definitions. They are announcing the third. Under that definition, the other two are beside the point before the argument begins.

What the cognitive position cannot survive is contact with its own logic. A nested hierarchy of TOTE units is as dependent on a prime mover as the reflex arc it was designed to replace. It cannot compel the "Image" to read itself. It cannot survive the Map-Reader problem, the philosophical doctrine proving that a map requires a navigator. The founders of the cognitive revolution identified the gap between the stimulus and the response, then built their remedy out of the same material as the problem: a mechanical structure that cannot see past its own highest node.

More damaging still, the digital-to-analogue conversion required to turn a symbolic Plan into a smooth biological movement was not a minor technical hurdle. It was an ontological chasm. The discrete, digital commands of the cortex must be smoothed into analogue action by the cerebellum. The logical gap was an established arrangement. The theory described the structure of behavior but smuggled the initiator through the backdoor. The question the cognitivists asked the behaviorists was the question they owed their own architecture first.

The cybernetic revolution was the statute the cognitivists used to designate behaviorism a structural risk. Applied to the human mind, the designation requires a significant interpretive leap: that biological intention, the messy metabolic reality of survival, constitutes an unwanted variable capable of degrading the elegance of the information channel. The move is philosophically aggressive and strategically deliberate. By establishing that intelligence is just data management, the cognitivists claimed a veto over the mystery of consciousness. Resistance was not a principled position available to the behaviorists. It was obsolescence they could see coming.

If a cybernetic loop cannot constrain the homunculus, and the Will can be theoretically reframed as a working memory buffer, then the cognitive architecture the psychological industry spent a century constructing was never biological. It was bureaucratic. The cognitive revolution did not abandon its ghosts under pressure. It exchanged a fragile, mystical form of agency for something more durable: a processing hierarchy. A mind whose operations are woven into feedback loops and list structures is not a mind the establishment can afford to doubt. It is an apparatus. An apparatus does not get to choose. It also does not stop running until the test is passed.

The measurements were taken before the hardware changed. They describe a computational mind adapting to a different reality than the biological one arriving now. When the feedback loop breaks, the question is not whether the plan was detailed enough. The question is who decided what the standard of the test was, and when they decided the organism no longer had the standing to ask.

### The Conduit and the Plasma: A Struggle Over Who Gets to Connect

The current fight over sociology is staged as a debate about structure, agency, and the "macro" forces that govern human behavior. It is not. It is a struggle over the physical existence of reality in a world where the visible signs of connection can be abstracted at scale. The first wave of sociological theory focused on "society," on whether the invisible forces of class or culture provided the context for human action. That conflict was real, and it revealed a structural fracture about what actually causes us to behave. But the second wave, initiated by Bruno Latour’s *Reassembling the Social*, is more destabilizing. It has moved from the context to the conduit, from the assumption of structure to the agonizing tracing of specific connections, from the question of what society *is* to the question of who is required to assemble it.

For a century, the sociological class secured its authority through the friction of abstraction. The theorist proved value by identifying the hidden "social forces" that the actors themselves could not see. The academic justified status by invoking capital-P "Power" or "Neoliberalism" as causal agents stored in a theoretical reservoir. The student earned apprenticeship by doing the repetitive labor of categorizing specific human actions into pre-existing structural boxes. Difficulty authenticated importance.
